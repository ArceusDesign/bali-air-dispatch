In this issue · Three readings from the unmonitored island
— Dedicated to the sensors that failed, & those that never were.
Cover feature · §1

Two in three days, the air exceeds the daily guideline.I

A community sensor in Kerobokan logged four hundred and sixty-nine days of Bali's breath before it fell silent — and on sixty-eight per cent of them, the twenty-four-hour average crossed a line the World Health Organization drew five years ago and that Indonesia has yet to recognise in law.

By The Bali Air Review · Staff
12-minute read
Kerobokan · 16 April 2026

The only long-running public record of Bali's air is a single community instrument on the back of a house in Kerobokan. It is a GAIA-class laser-scatter sensor, corrected by the AQICN network, and since November 2024 it has posted a daily median to a page almost no one reads. Across its 469 days of continuous operation, the mean reading reached 21.9 µg/m³ of PM2.5 — more than four times the World Health Organization's 5 µg/m³ annual guideline, and, on two of every three days, above the 15 µg/m³ ceiling the WHO set for a twenty-four-hour window. On the fifteenth of March 2026, without announcement, it fell silent.

§ A note on the sensor The Kerobokan unit is a GAIA A12, a low-cost laser-scatter device whose corrected values AQICN publishes as indicative. It is not a reference-grade monitor. It is, however, the only public long-record on the island.

The WHO's 2021 global air-quality guidelines lowered the twenty-four-hour PM2.5 threshold from 25 µg/m³ — where it had sat since 2005 — to 15. The tightening reflected a body of epidemiological evidence that had grown through the intervening decade: even moderate exposure, researchers now believed, carried cardiovascular and respiratory harm that the earlier guideline had not credited. Indonesia's national ambient standard, codified in Peraturan Pemerintah 22 of 2021, remained far looser at 55 µg/m³. Under domestic law, most of Bali's bad-air days were still normal.

"A single community sensor is not a regulatory finding. It is, however, the only count anyone has been keeping." — Field Note · §I

There are two difficulties with the Kerobokan record. The first is the instrument itself: a laser-scatter device will drift against a federal-equivalent reference monitor, and its absolute numbers, however carefully corrected, carry a slack of perhaps a few micrograms per cubic metre in either direction. The second difficulty is the harder one. No one has built a reference-grade record against which the community figures could be checked. The island has one such government instrument — in Denpasar — and through 2025 it reported sporadically, and then not at all.

The population-weighted annual PM2.5 for Indonesia in 2024, according to IQAir's World Air Quality Report, was 35.5 µg/m³, placing the country fifteenth in the world. Bali's annual mean sits lower than Jakarta's. It is not the worst air in the country. But the 5 µg/m³ guideline was written at that level for a reason, and on an island that sells its climate as the product, two-thirds of days above 15 is a number that deserves to be recorded. The Kerobokan sensor was, at the time of writing, the only one keeping count.

What does two in three days mean, lived? It means a child born in Kerobokan on the day the sensor began breathing the Bali average has, by the time she reaches a first birthday, spent twice the WHO-recommended annual dose. It means that on any given week, four of its days will meet the short-term ceiling an international body considers a line of public health consequence — and three of those four will exceed it. It means that the burden, where burden is measurable, is already laid. The missing instrument is not an instrument of protection. It is an instrument of record.

Record is the quiet thing that is absent. When the Denpasar sensor reports, it publishes an hourly AQI and a twenty-four-hour PM2.5 reading; when it does not, there is nothing on the national ISPU dashboard for the island. A 17,000-island archipelago cannot be air-quality-measured by inference. And yet, on the island's one long private record, the daily guideline has been routinely exceeded for a year and a half. What follows reports three conditions of that absence — three articles about three kinds of missing.

§II · The coverage gap
One.

One government sensor for an island of 4.4 million.

Bali's projected 2025 population, according to BPS Statistics Indonesia, is 4.46 million. The number of KLHK reference-grade PM2.5 monitors serving it — indexed on the national ISPU portal — is one. That sensor, at Denpasar Lumintang on Jalan Mulawarman, has reported sporadically through 2025 and, as of the first quarter of 2026, has not appeared on the portal in months.

Jakarta, for comparison, operates 10 reference-grade sensors and some 110 low-cost community monitors through its DKI programme, according to the 2024 Indonesia Air Quality report from the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air. Hong Kong runs 18 fixed stations for 7.5 million residents; London more than 100 on its LAQN network. On a per-capita basis, Bali's figure is roughly 0.2 stations per million. Jakarta's is 11. London's is 11. Hong Kong's is 2.4.

The CREA report, describing provincial coverage outside Jakarta, called it "a significant coverage gap beyond the city limits into other satellite cities." Bali is one of those gaps. When the Denpasar instrument is running, it speaks. When it is not, the island's official record is silent — not because there are no pollutants to measure, but because there is no government instrument to measure them with.

§III · The unintended consequence
Seven.

Seven tons of river waste a day, and no air data to match.

On the first of April 2026 the new Denpasar waste rule took effect: no sorted waste, no collection. The Badung regency attached a penalty of up to Rp 25 million and three months' imprisonment. The rule's stated purpose was to force the separation stream that would allow the long-troubled Suwung landfill — handling roughly 1,000 tonnes a day — to stop accepting organics, and eventually, on 1 August, to close.

Two weeks in, the consequence is a different one. Denpasar's Public Works and Spatial Planning Office reported in mid-April that up to seven tons of waste a day were being pulled from the city's rivers by municipal crews — a figure attributed by Ketut Ngurah Artha Jaya, the office's head of water resources. The river-cleanup organisation Sungai Watch, which separately processes about three tons of plastic a day at its headquarters, has flagged a visible post-1-April surge.

Open household burning is a recognised, locally dominant PM2.5 source in Indonesian communities with incomplete collection coverage, according to the Climate & Clean Air Coalition's CLOCC programme. The science is old; the policy mechanism is obvious. What is new, and specific to Bali, is that the burning has begun to rise — visible from the alleys in Sanur and Kuta, measurable at the AQICN community sensors — and no government instrument is publishing a number against which the rise can be recorded.

Editor's note

The air is being breathed without record.

A sensor is not a solution. It is a witness. Its absence does not lower the dose the body receives; it lowers only the public's capacity to reason about it. What Bali lacks, in April of 2026, is not clean air — air of any quality, clean or not, is not a negotiable good — but the means by which its cleanliness, or the failure of it, can be known to the people who breathe it.

That is a small, recoverable gap. It asks for a handful of reference-grade monitors, an honest public feed, and a continuity of record. It asks for little money on the scale of a provincial budget. It has not, yet, been asked for loudly.
