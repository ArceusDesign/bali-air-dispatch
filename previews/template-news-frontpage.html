Cover story · Air quality

Two in three days, Bali's air exceeds the daily guideline.

A community sensor in Kerobokan logged 469 days of the island's breath before it fell silent on 15 March. For most of them, the reading was above the line the World Health Organization now sets for a single day's exposure.

The Bali Air Dispatch · Staff Filed · 16 April 2026 12-minute read

The only long-running public record of Bali's air is a single community instrument on the back of a house in Kerobokan. It is a GAIA-class laser-scatter sensor corrected by the AQICN network, and since November 2024 it has posted a daily median to a page almost no one reads. Across its 469 days of operation, the mean reading reached 21.9 µg/m³ of PM2.5 — more than four times the World Health Organization's 5 µg/m³ annual guideline, and on two of every three days above the 15 µg/m³ ceiling the WHO set for a single day's exposure. On the fifteenth of March 2026, without announcement, it went dark.

The WHO's 2021 guidelines lowered the 24-hour PM2.5 threshold from 25 µg/m³ — where it had sat since 2005 — to 15. The tightening reflected epidemiological evidence that even moderate exposure carried cardiovascular and respiratory harm the earlier guideline had not credited. Indonesia's national ambient standard, codified in PP 22/2021, remained at 55 µg/m³. Under domestic law, most of Bali's bad-air days are still normal.

There are two difficulties with the Kerobokan record. The first is the instrument — a laser-scatter device drifts against reference-grade monitors. The second is the harder one: no-one has built a reference-grade record against which the community figures could be checked. The island has one such government instrument, in Denpasar, and through 2025 it reported sporadically, and then not at all.

The coverage gap

Infrastructure · §IIBy the numbers · 17 Apr 2026
By the numbers

One government sensor, four and a half million breathers.

On a per-capita basis, Bali runs roughly 0.2 reference-grade PM2.5 stations per million residents. Jakarta runs eleven. London runs eleven. Hong Kong runs two-and-a-half. Bali is outside the frame.

Bali's projected 2025 population, according to BPS Statistics Indonesia, is 4.46 million. The number of KLHK reference-grade PM2.5 monitors serving it — indexed on the national ISPU portal — is one. That sensor, at Denpasar Lumintang on Jalan Mulawarman, reported sporadically through 2025 and, as of early 2026, has not appeared on the portal in months.

Jakarta, for comparison, operates 10 reference-grade sensors and some 110 low-cost community monitors through its DKI programme, according to the 2024 Indonesia Air Quality report from the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air (CREA). Hong Kong operates 18 fixed stations for 7.5 million residents; London more than 100 on its Local Air Quality Network alone.

The CREA report described provincial coverage outside Jakarta as "a significant coverage gap beyond the city limits into other satellite cities." Bali is one of those gaps. When the Denpasar instrument is running, it speaks. When it is not, the island's official record is silent.

The unintended consequence

Waste & air · §IIIApril 2026
Post-ban reality

Seven tons a day in the rivers, and smoke in the alleys.

Since 1 April 2026, Denpasar will not collect unsorted waste. The rule aims to close the Suwung landfill. Its early consequence, visibly, is rising household burning and rivers filling with plastic.

On the first of April 2026 the new Denpasar waste rule took effect: no sorted waste, no collection. In Badung, the penalty reached Rp 25 million and three months' imprisonment. The stated purpose was to force the separation stream that would let Suwung — handling around 1,000 tonnes a day — stop accepting organics, and eventually close on 1 August.

Two weeks in, the consequence was a different one. Denpasar's Public Works and Spatial Planning Office (PUPR) reported that up to seven tons of waste a day were being pulled from the city's rivers by municipal crews — a figure attributed to Ketut Ngurah Artha Jaya, head of water resources. Sungai Watch, which separately processes about three tons of plastic daily, flagged a visible post-1-April surge.

Open household burning is a recognised, locally dominant PM2.5 source in Indonesian communities with incomplete collection coverage, per the Climate & Clean Air Coalition's CLOCC programme for Indonesia. Community sensors in Kuta, Canggu and Uluwatu logged US-AQI spikes as high as 150 in the first two weeks of April — the threshold at which "unhealthy for everyone" begins. No government data exists to corroborate them.

Editorial · §E1

The air is being breathed without record.

A sensor is not a solution. It is a witness. Its absence does not lower the dose the body receives; it lowers only the public's capacity to reason about it. What Bali lacks, in April of 2026, is not clean air — air of any quality is not a negotiable good — but the means by which its cleanliness, or the failure of it, can be known to the people who breathe it.

That is a small, recoverable gap. It asks for a handful of reference-grade monitors, an honest public feed, and a continuity of record. It asks for little money on the scale of a provincial budget. It has not, yet, been asked for loudly.

— The Editors
