Chapter I

The monitoring collapse

One government station now covers an island of 4.4 million people. Two other long-running sensors went silent in the months before a major waste regulation took effect.

In August 2025, the government air-quality station at Graha Sewaka Dharma in central Denpasar went dark. Its last two daily readings climbed sharply — into the Unhealthy band on the US EPA scale — and then the feed stopped. Eight months later, the station remains listed on the KLHK portal as Sedang dalam perawatan: under maintenance. Searches of the portal for "Denpasar," "Bali," or "Gianyar" return nothing.

In March 2026, the community sensor at Kerobokan — operated privately, shared through the AQICN network — stopped transmitting. Its 469 days of record form the longest continuous, openly downloadable window we have into the air above south Bali. It stopped two weeks before the 1 April waste ban under which Denpasar barred organic material from the Suwung landfill.

What remains is the Sempidi station in Kabupaten Badung, one private aggregator network with thirteen stations scattered across the southern regencies, and two PurpleAir community units on the coasts. No multi-year public record exists for Sanur, Amed, East or North Bali. No open-data feed is published by the provincial government.

Chapter II

The record, such as it is

Five historical sensors, two networks, and two still-reporting PurpleAir community units. This is the totality of the public archive.

68%
of 469 Kerobokan days exceeded the WHO 24-hour guideline

The Kerobokan sensor recorded 469 days of data from November 2024 to March 2026. The average daily median was 21.9 µg/m³ — 4.4 times the WHO 2021 annual guideline of 5 µg/m³. During its 166-day overlap with the Denpasar station, readings ran 1.1–1.7 times higher, suggesting real, measurable geographic variation within south Bali.

A short-record sensor at Kopernik / Ubud recorded 36 days from October 2025 to March 2026. The daily median averaged 31.2 µg/m³ — over six times the WHO annual guideline. Ninety-two percent of recorded days exceeded the 24-hour target. The sensor peaked at 111 µg/m³ on 26 February 2026 and went dark in mid-March.

Chapter III

April, 2026

The regulation is sound. Its execution has widened the very gap that monitoring was supposed to measure.

On the first of April, Denpasar authorities banned organic waste from the Suwung landfill — Bali's largest waste endpoint — as part of a plan to close the site by August 2026. The stated aim is a legitimate environmental objective: reduce landfill dependency, push households toward source separation.

Implementation has outpaced infrastructure. Without adequate composting or collection alternatives in place, residents have in some cases turned to burning waste in backyards and roadsides, and in others to dumping it in the rivers that feed the west-coast catchments. Denpasar cleanup crews have reportedly removed approximately seven tons of waste from rivers daily, according to local reporting citing the city's Public Works office (PUPR).

Mixed-waste burning — particularly of plastics — releases PM2.5 alongside dioxins, furans, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and heavy metals. The concern addressed here is not the regulation itself. It is that Bali currently has no instrumentation adequate to measure the air-quality consequences of a major waste-management transition as it unfolds.

Chapter V

What would help

Four practical, low-cost actions that any actor — governmental, philanthropic, commercial, or individual — could take or fund now.

  1. Restore the Denpasar government station.

    The Lumintang KLHK station produced almost two years of valuable data before going offline. Repairing or replacing a single monitor is not a major infrastructure investment. A published return-to-service date would itself be useful.

  2. Deploy a community sensor network.

    Low-cost open-hardware monitors such as AirGradient (US$195) and PurpleAir (US$229) can fill gaps immediately. A network of five to ten units across populated Bali would transform visibility at a total cost well under US$3,000.

  3. Publish whatever is already being measured.

    Any operational sensor — government, hospitality, industrial, community — should feed an open platform such as OpenAQ, AQICN, or PurpleAir. Private data makes private decisions. Public data makes public ones possible.

  4. Measure the waste-rule transition in real time.

    As Bali moves away from the Suwung landfill, live air-quality data is the only feedback loop that can catch burning hotspots early — and the only one that can distinguish regulatory success from regulatory blowback.

An Editorial · 17 April 2026

Bali's air is being breathed without record. The data we do not have is itself the argument.
