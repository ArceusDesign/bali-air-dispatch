A quiet crisis, politely unmeasured.

Bali's sole urban government air quality station has been offline for eight months. Seven private sensors tell a consistent, uncomfortable story — one the province is not formally keeping records of.

01Context

After the landfill closed its gates.

In April 2026, the provincial government tightened its position on organic waste: Suwung, Bali's primary landfill for decades, stopped accepting organic material. The rule was described as a step toward better waste management. In the weeks that followed, residents across Ubud, Kerobokan, Canggu and Jimbaran reported a visible increase in burning — in households, at compound edges, along rivers, in the early morning and after dark.

A regulation is only as useful as the infrastructure that supports compliance. When organic waste cannot go to the landfill and composting is not widely available, it does not disappear. It burns — and PM2.5 is what it leaves in the air.

02What the record shows

Seven sensors, two networks, one picture.

Public historical data exists for only a handful of Bali air quality monitors. The AQICN network preserves records for the Kerobokan community sensor (469 days, now offline) and the Denpasar government KLHK station (548 days, offline since August 2025). OpenAQ preserves CSVs for three additional private AirGradient sensors in Ubud, Balangan and Kopernik — all now offline as well.

Two PurpleAir units in Klungkung and Jimbaran are still reporting. Together these seven sensors produce an uneven, patchy, but consistently sobering picture: most days in populated Bali exceed the WHO 24-hour guideline. Some months, almost all days do.

Section 09
Without distributed monitoring, it is impossible to determine whether the patterns seen in Kerobokan and Denpasar are representative of the island as a whole.
