Investigation Air Quality · Bali · April 2026

After the waste ban, the air is unmeasured.

Bali's only government air-quality station for 4.4 million residents has been offline for eight months. The community sensor that filled the gap across the Canggu corridor went silent two weeks before the 1 April landfill rules took effect. This is what the record shows — and what it cannot.

§ II — Regulation

A rule that outran its infrastructure.

On 1 April 2026, Denpasar banned organic waste from the Suwung landfill — Bali's largest waste endpoint — as part of a plan to close the site by August 2026. The stated aim was a legitimate environmental objective: push households toward source separation and reduce landfill dependency.

Implementation has outpaced infrastructure. Without adequate composting or collection alternatives in place, some residents have turned to burning waste in backyards and roadsides; others to dumping it in the rivers feeding the west-coast catchments. Denpasar cleanup crews have reportedly removed approximately seven tons of waste from rivers daily, per the city's Public Works office.

What burns at roadside does not appear in any such tally. It appears only in the air.

§ III — Silence

Two sensors, silent by March's end.

The Kerobokan community sensor (AQICN / GAIA) stopped transmitting on 15 March 2026. It had run for 469 consecutive days. Two weeks later, Denpasar's organic-waste ban took effect.

The Kopernik / Ubud AirGradient (OpenAQ) stopped on 16 March 2026, mid-month. It had run for 36 days; 92% of them had exceeded the WHO 24-hour guideline. Its peak of 111 µg/m³ on 26 February placed it firmly in the "Unhealthy" band on the US EPA scale.

Both were privately operated volunteer sensors. As such, they can go offline for any number of reasons — failure, relocation, choice. The problem is not that any one sensor went dark. The problem is that no public backstop exists.

§ V

What would help

Four actions · low cost · available now
  1. Action 01

    Restore the Denpasar government station.

    The Lumintang KLHK station produced almost two years of valuable data before going offline. Repairing or replacing a single monitor is not a major infrastructure investment.

  2. Action 02

    Deploy a community sensor network.

    AirGradient (US$195) and PurpleAir (US$229) can fill gaps immediately. Five to ten units across populated Bali would transform visibility at a total cost under US$3,000.

  3. Action 03

    Publish whatever is already being measured.

    Any operational sensor — government, hospitality, industrial, community — should feed an open platform. Private data helps private decisions; public data makes public ones possible.

  4. Action 04

    Measure the waste-rule transition in real time.

    As Bali moves away from the Suwung landfill, live air-quality data is the only feedback loop that can catch burning hotspots early — and the only one that can distinguish regulatory success from blowback.
