Friday, the seventeenth of April, two thousand twenty-six · Ubud, Bali
Essay no. one

The Particulate, & the Silence

Filed from Ubud · Correspondence to baliair@protonmail.com

In April of 2026, the provincial government of Bali issued a small regulation with a large effect: the Suwung landfill, for years the island's primary endpoint for organic waste, would stop accepting it. The rule was presented as a step toward better waste management. It was. It was also — in practice, and without the infrastructure to absorb the shift — an invitation to burn. Note I See the Hey Bali News report of late March 2026: seven tonnes of daily waste now entering Bali's rivers, household burning rising in Denpasar and Canggu. The present essay does not attempt to adjudicate cause; it documents what the sensors saw.

Within weeks, residents across Ubud, Kerobokan, Canggu and Jimbaran reported a visible increase in smoke — at compound edges, along rivers, in the early morning, and at dusk. Such accounts are anecdotes; they are also, individually, unfalsifiable. To ask whether the air is worse than it was is a question for instruments, not impressions.

What follows is an inventory of the instruments presently in place across the island, of what they say, and — perhaps more importantly — of what, collectively, they cannot.

Seven sensors, two networks

Public, downloadable historical air quality data for Bali exists for exactly seven sensors, on two open platforms. The AQICN network preserves records for the Kerobokan community sensor, which operated from November 2024 until March 2026 and now sits quiet, and for the Denpasar government KLHK station, which operated from September 2023 until a sharp, unexplained spike on the 9th of August 2025 and has not reported since. Between them, these two sensors produced just over a thousand days of record.

OpenAQ preserves histories for three private AirGradient sensors: one in Ubud (Rozendal, 167 days), one at Balangan (26 days), and one at Kopernik in central Ubud (36 days, merged from two co-located units). All three are now offline. Two PurpleAir units in Klungkung and Jimbaran are still reporting — nearly two years of continuous data between them.

One response to such figures is to object: a single sensor cannot speak for a neighbourhood, let alone an island. That is correct. It is also the essay's central point. The problem is not that the numbers are alarming; it is that there are so few of them. Note II There is no multi-year public record for Sanur, for North Bali, or for East Bali outside a handful of resort-adjacent private sensors. The conclusions that follow from the available data are, of necessity, partial.

What the numbers do, and do not, show

Across the record, a pattern recurs. The Kerobokan sensor's mean PM2.5 over 469 days was 21.9 µg/m³ — 4.4 times the WHO annual guideline. Sixty-eight percent of its recorded days exceeded the 24-hour guideline. The Denpasar station, in its own record of 548 days, averaged 15.1 µg/m³; about 40% of days exceeded the 24-hour guideline. The OpenAQ AirGradient sensors, though shorter in tenure, track the same range.

What one cannot conclude from the above is whether the patterns described are representative of Bali as a whole, or whether particulate concentrations have risen, fallen, or held steady since the April regulation. The record is too thin, the geography too patchy, and the gaps — Sanur, Ubud for the months since December, North Bali at all — too many.

Without distributed monitoring, it is impossible to determine whether the patterns above are representative of the island.

An argument for more instruments

The instruments exist. A consumer-grade PurpleAir sensor costs around USD 229; an AirGradient Open Air costs USD 195. A network of ten such devices, distributed across populated Bali and publishing to an open API, would transform what we are able to say. The cost is small; the institutional will, presently, is absent. That is the gap this document is here to describe.

A second essay, forthcoming, will examine what a minimal distributed network would cost to operate, who might host it, and what guarantees of independence such a project would require. For now, the record — incomplete, unevenly distributed, and quietly alarming — is what is public.
