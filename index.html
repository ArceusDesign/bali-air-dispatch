Evidence Brief · Public Health

Bali's Invisible Air Quality Crisis

Date: April 13, 2026 Prepared by: Anonymous concerned resident Purpose: Evidence for journalists and investigators
As of April 13, 2026: Bali's only government air quality station (Denpasar) has been offline since August 2025. Limited private monitoring exists (Nafas: 4 stations; IQAir contributors: ~10 sensors; PurpleAir: 1 active), but there are no official government sensors operating in Ubud, Kerobokan, Canggu, or central Denpasar — the areas most affected by recent waste burning concerns.
Section 1

Summary

Independent air quality sensor data from Bali shows that residents and tourists have been breathing dangerously polluted air for at least 16 months. On 320 out of 469 recorded days, fine particulate matter (PM2.5) exceeded the World Health Organization's annual guideline. On 107 of those days, pollution spikes reached levels classified as "Hazardous" by the US EPA — the worst category, where all people are at serious health risk.

Then the sensors went dark.

The only independent air quality sensor in the Kerobokan/Canggu area stopped recording on March 15, 2026 — two weeks before a controversial regulation change that has made the burning problem even worse. The Indonesian government's own air quality station in Denpasar went offline eight months earlier in August 2025 and has been quietly removed from the national monitoring portal.

Section 2

The Regulation That Backfired

On April 1, 2026, Denpasar authorities banned organic waste from the Suwung landfill — Bali's largest and only major waste processing site — as part of a plan to close it by August 2026. The stated goal was to push households toward waste separation.

The reality: Without adequate composting infrastructure or collection alternatives, residents turned to burning waste and dumping it in rivers. Denpasar cleanup crews now remove approximately 7 tons of waste from rivers daily, according to the city's Public Works and Spatial Planning Office (PUPR).

Waste burning — especially of mixed waste containing plastics — releases toxic particulate matter, dioxins, furans, and other carcinogens into the air. Standard PM2.5 sensors only capture one dimension of this health risk; the actual exposure is likely worse than the numbers suggest.

Section 5

What Monitoring Exists Today

As of April 13, 2026, a comprehensive audit of all publicly accessible air quality monitoring platforms reveals severe gaps across Bali:

Ubud — a town of approximately 75,000 residents and a major international tourist destination — has exactly one air quality sensor, operated by the private company Nafas. There is no government monitoring, no independent monitoring, and no redundancy.

Kuta, Denpasar, and Canggu — Bali's most densely populated areas and the areas most affected by waste burning — have no functioning independent air quality sensors.

Section 6

The Systemic Problem: Indonesia's Neglected Monitoring Infrastructure

This is not only a Bali problem. In August 2024, Riau Online reported that air quality monitoring equipment in Pekanbaru, Riau Province, had been broken for years — with equipment failing progressively since 2016 and only one of three stations still operational.

Indonesia's national AQMS network has historically struggled with maintenance and calibration across its roughly 39 stations. The result is that when environmental crises occur, the infrastructure to measure their impact often does not exist.

Section 7

Understanding PM2.5: Why This Matters for Health

PM2.5 refers to fine particulate matter smaller than 2.5 micrometers — small enough to penetrate deep into the lungs and enter the bloodstream. Long-term exposure is linked to respiratory disease, cardiovascular disease, lung cancer, and premature death. Short-term exposure to high concentrations causes immediate respiratory distress, especially in children, elderly people, and those with pre-existing conditions.

When waste containing plastics is burned, the smoke contains not just PM2.5 but also dioxins, furans, hydrogen chloride, and other toxic compounds that standard PM sensors do not measure. The actual health risk from waste burning smoke is likely higher than PM2.5 numbers alone suggest.
